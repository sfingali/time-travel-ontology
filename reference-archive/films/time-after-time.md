# Time After Time (1979)

## Short blurb
In 1893 London, H. G. Wells’s friend John Leslie Stevenson—Jack the Ripper—steals Wells’s time machine to 1979 San Francisco. Wells follows, falls for bank clerk Amy Robbins, and must stop a killer who finds the violent twentieth century a playground.

## Detailed plot summary (spoilers)

Wells demonstrates his machine to dinner guests, explaining a **non-return key** (holds the machine at the destination) and a **vaporizing equalizer** (keeps traveler and machine paired). Police arrive hunting the Ripper; bloody gloves implicate surgeon Stevenson. The machine is gone—Stevenson fled without the non-return key, so it autoprovides back to 1893. Wells pursues to **5 November 1979**, finding the machine exhibited in a San Francisco museum.

Utopian socialist Wells is crushed by twentieth-century war and greed. A string of unsolved murders of women suggests Stevenson is hunting again. Wells exchanges antiquated currency, navigates McDonald’s, and tracks bank exchanges to Amy Robbins, who earlier directed Stevenson to the Hyatt. Confronted, Stevenson revels: “Ninety years ago I was a freak. Today I’m an amateur.” He attacks for the key, is hit by a car, and escapes a hospital that lies to Wells.

Amy and Wells romance; Stevenson frightens Amy at the bank and learns her address. To prove his story, Wells takes her **three days forward**; a newspaper names her the fifth “San Francisco Ripper” victim. They return aiming to stop the fourth murder, are delayed, and can only phone police. Wells is framed for the fourth killing; Amy is stalked. Police find a dismembered body in her apartment (her coworker, not Amy), release Wells, then Stevenson demands the key with Amy as hostage.

At the museum Stevenson boards the machine with Amy. Wells removes the vaporizing equalizer as Stevenson starts it—casting Stevenson across the continuum while the machine stays. Wells must return to 1893 and destroy the device; Amy, already “legally dead,” insists on coming. Caption: Wells married Amy Catherine Robbins (d. 1927); he anticipated socialism, global war, space travel, women’s liberation; died 1946.

## Beat-by-beat temporal structure

1. **1893 demo & Ripper reveal.**
2. **Stevenson theft (no key) → machine returns empty.**
3. **Wells to 1979 museum / culture shock.**
4. **Bank trail → Amy; Stevenson’s modernity boast.**
5. **Three-day proof jump** (Amy’s future headline).
4. **Frame-up / hostage exchange.**
5. **Equalizer sabotage** strands Stevenson in non-time.
6. **Coupled return to Victorian marriage.**

Meyer developed the film from ~55 pages of Alexander’s then-unpublished novel. McDowell avoided Wells’s real high squeaky accent for dignity. Warner beat studio interest in Mick Jagger. Filmed across San Francisco landmarks. Miklós Rózsa score (Saturn Award).

## Paradox analysis

- **Hardware rules matter:** Non-return key and equalizer are plot locks, not handwaves.
- **Pursuit TT:** Same vehicle, opposing moral projects (utopia vs predation).
- **Future as indictment:** Stevenson’s pleasure in 1979 violence critiques Wells’s progress narrative.
- **Romance exit:** Amy emigrates permanently to 1893—closed personal ending, open historical caption.
- **Kinship to Meyer’s *Star Trek IV*:** Currency fish-out-of-water gags in modern San Francisco.

Steenburgen later mirrors the cross-century romance from the other direction in *Back to the Future Part III*.

## Endings / cuts / production notes

Orion / Warner; TIFF premiere; ~$13M box office. RT ~88%. Saturn Awards: Meyer (writing), Steenburgen, Rózsa. 2016 ABC series adaptation canceled after five episodes.
