# About Time (2013)

## Short blurb
Cornwall barrister Tim Lake learns that men in his family can travel to moments they have already lived. He rewrites dating mishaps and family crises until conception physics and his father’s death force him to stop rewinding and live each day once.

## Detailed plot summary (spoilers)

Tim grows up in Cornwall with father James (Bill Nighy), mother Mary, uncle Desmond, and sister Kit Kat. The morning after a disappointing New Year’s party, James reveals that Lake men can travel back in time to any moment of their own past by concentrating in a dark space (a cupboard, under covers). Fame and fortune are discouraged as boring to prior relatives; Tim decides to use the gift for love.

Summer crush Charlotte (Margot Robbie) remains uninterested no matter when he confesses—proof that travel cannot rewrite another person’s will. Tim moves to London, rooms with playwright Harry (Tom Hollander), and meets American publisher Mary (Rachel McAdams) at a dark restaurant (Dans le Noir). After he rewinds to save Harry’s opening night, the Mary evening never occurred; he must re-meet her at a Kate Moss exhibition, then rewind again to arrive before a rival boyfriend. Their relationship sticks; he declines a later Charlotte advance and marries Mary. Daughter Posy is born.

Kit Kat’s toxic relationship and crash on Posy’s birthday push Tim to intervene: he prevents the crash and even takes Kit Kat into the past to avert the bad romance. Returning, Posy has never been born—he has a son instead. James explains that changing events before a child’s conception can replace which child is conceived. Tim restores the crash timeline so Posy exists, then helps Kit Kat in the present (she settles with friend Jay). A son follows.

James develops terminal lung cancer. Travel cannot undo smoking without undoing Tim and Kit Kat’s conceptions. James has been secretly extending his remaining days by replaying them. He advises Tim to live each day twice: first with ordinary tensions, second noticing sweetness. After James dies, Tim visits him one last time in the past. When Mary wants a third child, Tim knows further travel past that conception will cut him off from James forever; father and son share one carefully unchanged childhood memory. A third child (a girl) arrives. Tim finally abandons travel altogether, trying to treat ordinary life as if it were already the second pass.

## Beat-by-beat temporal structure

1. **Revelation & rules:** Male-line gift; only one’s lived past; no mind-control of others (Charlotte beat).
2. **London courtship loops:** Harry’s play vs Mary’s number; exhibition re-meet; boyfriend preemption.
3. **Marriage & Posy:** Stable present; gift mostly idle.
4. **Kit Kat rewrite catastrophe:** Pre-conception edit → Posy replaced by a son; rule made visceral.
5. **Acceptance of sister’s agency:** Crash restored; present-tense help.
6. **James’s illness:** Irreversible without erasing children; day-doubling ethic.
7. **Third child cutoff:** Last shared memory with father; travel abandoned.

Curtis has called the film an “anti–time travel movie”: the device dramatizes cherishing ordinary time rather than solving life with rewinds. Critics (Time magazine, Film School Rejects, Mark Kermode) note soft or inconsistent rules—especially late emotional exceptions—relative to hard SF.

## Paradox analysis

- **Branching / rewrite:** Each rewind replaces Tim’s experienced present; others’ memories update unless they share the gift (James).
- **Conception butterfly:** Pre-birth edits change *which* child arrives—strongest hard constraint in the film.
- **Limits that aren’t physics:** Cannot change others’ feelings; cannot undo father’s cancer without undoing offspring.
- **Not closed-loop:** Tim successfully alters many personal outcomes; the arc is toward *refusing* further alteration.
- **Soft rule criticism:** Reviewers flag moments where stated limits seem bent for hugs or theme (e.g., post-death visits while still planning a third child).

## Endings / cuts / production notes

Working Title / Universal; Curtis’s third feature as director (he suggested it might be his last). Effects kept deliberately low-key after spectacle tests felt wrong tonally. Dedicated to Richard Griffiths (final film appearance, uncredited stage actor). Strong South Korean box office relative to other foreign rom-coms. Soundtrack: Nick Laird-Clowes plus curated pop (The Cure, Killers, Amy Winehouse, etc.).

## Comparative notes / public commentary

TIME magazine and Film School Rejects essays catalog soft-rule complaints: Charlotte’s will as absolute; conception butterfly as absolute; then late emotional visits that seem to bend the cutoff. Curtis has answered in interviews that the film is deliberately an “anti–time travel movie”—the gift’s purpose is to teach Tim to stop using it. Bill Nighy’s James becomes the thematic center: a man who has already learned to live days twice and who models refusal. Domhnall Gleeson’s performance tracks from opportunistic rewriter to present-tense father.

Box-office and reception notes from public sources: strong UK/international romantic comedy returns; outsized South Korean popularity among imported rom-coms of its year. Soundtrack curation (Nick Laird-Clowes supervision) leans on needle-drops that mark “ordinary” days as already special. Working Title craft places it beside Curtis’s *Love Actually* / *Notting Hill* emotional grammar with a single SF lever.

Teaching use in this archive: pair with *Frequency* (father–son temporal intimacy without shared travel) and *The Time Traveler’s Wife* (involuntary jumps vs chosen cupboard travel). Contrast *Primer*’s broken symmetry: here asymmetry of the gift (only Lake men) is accepted family lore, not a betrayal engine.

## Additional sourced beats (Wikipedia / reception)

Public plot summaries stress the Dans le Noir first meeting, the Kate Moss exhibition re-meet after Harry’s play wipe, and the rival-boyfriend preemption as the courtship trilogy. Kit Kat’s crash-on-Posy’s-birthday intervention is the hard lesson: restoring the crash restores Posy. James’s day-doubling advice and the final shared childhood memory before the third-child cutoff are the emotional climax most reviews cite. Curtis dedicated the film in part to savoring ordinary time; Richard Griffiths’s final appearance is uncredited on stage. Critical conversation (TIME’s plot-hole essay; Kermode) treats rule softness as thematic, not only sloppy—useful when teaching “emotional rewrite” TT versus Primer-hard accounting.
